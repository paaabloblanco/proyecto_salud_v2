## 1. Daily protein needs (g/kg/day) and whether they vary by type of day

### Takeaway
All major bodies converge on roughly **1.2–2.2 g/kg/day** for athletes, with ~**1.6 g/kg/day** as a well-supported practical target (Morton 2018 plateau; UEFA "~1.6" from 4 meals × 0.4 g/kg). Guidelines do **not** give separate protein figures per day type (match / training / rest); they say protein should be steady and spread across the day, and that day-to-day periodisation applies mainly to carbohydrate. Upper ranges (2.0–2.4+) are tied to energy restriction or injury, which are out of scope for the app.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM/AND/DC 2016: "dietary protein intake necessary to support metabolic adaptation, repair, remodeling, and for protein turnover generally ranges from **1.2 to 2.0 g/kg/d**. Higher intakes may be indicated for short periods during intensified training or when reducing energy intake." — [ACSM/AND/DC 2016 full text](https://sky.sausport.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/American-College-of-Sports-Medicine_Joint-Position_Nutrition_and_Athletic_Performance_2016.pdf)
- ACSM/AND/DC 2016 on day-type variation: "individuals should no longer be solely categorized as strength or endurance athletes and provided with static daily protein intake targets. Rather, guidelines should be based around optimal adaptation to specific sessions of training/competition within a periodized program". Requirements "can fluctuate based on 'trained' status (experienced athletes requiring less), training (sessions involving higher frequency and intensity, or a new training stimulus at higher end of protein range), carbohydrate availability, and most importantly, energy availability." No numeric per-day-type protein values are given. — [ACSM/AND/DC 2016](http://drugfreesport.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Position-stand-on-Nutrition-Athletic-Performance-ACSM-2016-1.pdf)
- ACSM/AND/DC 2016: energy restriction or sudden inactivity (injury): "elevated protein intakes as high as 2.0 g/kg/day or higher ... may be advantageous in preventing fat-free mass loss." — [ACSM/AND/DC 2016](http://drugfreesport.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Position-stand-on-Nutrition-Athletic-Performance-ACSM-2016-1.pdf)
- ISSN 2017 (protein): **1.4–2.0 g/kg/day** "is sufficient for most exercising individuals" to build/maintain muscle; 2.3–3.1 g/kg/d may be needed to preserve lean mass in resistance-trained people during hypocaloric periods; >3.0 g/kg/d may have effects on body composition in resistance-trained people. — [Jäger et al. 2017, PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/)
- UEFA 2021: "The RDA for protein is 0.8 g/kg BM/day in Europe, but higher intakes **up to 1.6–2.2 g/kg BM/day** appear to enhance training adaptation. Such levels of protein intake can easily be achieved from a mixed diet provided the energy intake is sufficient". Premier League players reported 2–2.5 g/kg/day; "protein supplements are probably not needed for most players". — [Collins et al. 2021, BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- UEFA 2021: energy restriction or rehabilitation: "a higher protein intake (perhaps **2.0–2.4 g/kg BM/day**) that is dependent on training load and other metabolic stresses, such as weight loss or rehabilitation from injury"; for injury rehab, "recommended daily protein intake being ≥1.6 g/kg BM". — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- UEFA 2021 places protein in the "training day" section; the post-match section gives protein as a **distribution** target (20–25 g every 3–4 h) rather than a separate daily g/kg figure. Day-to-day periodisation in UEFA is explicitly about carbohydrate ("how nutrient requirements (specifically carbohydrate) for training should change each day based on training demands"). — [UEFA press release](https://www.uefa.com/news-media/news/0262-10b3a43fd486-f4484edc6615-1000--uefa-launches-expert-group-statement-on-nutrition-for-elit/); [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- Ranchordas 2017 (congested fixtures, elite soccer): "During intensified periods of competition a recommended strategy of **1.5–2 g/kg body weight per day** (e.g. 120–160 g for 80 kg player) should be sufficient to fully repair damaged muscle"; abstract says protein ">1.5 g/kg". — [Ranchordas et al. 2017, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5596842)
- Morton 2018 (49 RCTs, 1,863 participants, healthy adults doing resistance training): "Protein supplementation beyond total protein intakes of **1.62 g/kg/day** resulted in no further RET-induced gains in FFM." Breakpoint = 1.62 (95% CI **1.03–2.20**) g/kg/day, p = 0.079 (not statistically significant); authors: "it may be prudent to recommend ~2.2 g protein/kg/d for those seeking to maximise resistance training-induced gains in FFM." — [Morton et al. 2018, PMC5867436](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867436/)
- Critique of the 1.6 plateau: the breakpoint did not reach significance, and later analyses with other methods (Tagawa et al. 2021 spline model; Nunes et al. 2022, 66 studies, 2,665 participants: <1.2 g/kg g = −0.14 n.s.; 1.2–1.59 g/kg g = 0.17; ≥1.6 g/kg g = 0.30) did not show a clear plateau at 1.6. — [OMNID secondary summary](https://www.omnidtraining.com/field-notes/nutrition-01-protein-intake) (secondary source; primary papers not read in this session)
- Futsal intakes: Azorean senior amateur futsal players (II Division, n = 20) averaged **1.9 ± 0.6 g/kg/day** protein (range 0.8–3.0), i.e. inside ISSN 1.4–2.0; carbohydrate (3.4 g/kg) was below target. — [Brum et al. 2023, PMC10055704](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10055704/). Top-level Polish futsal players (n = 11) had "slightly higher protein" than recommended and low carbohydrate. — [Gogojewicz et al. 2023, PMC10490003](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10490003/)

### Inferences
- A defensible rule-engine default for healthy adult amateur futsal players in energy balance: **1.6 g/kg/day** (range 1.2–2.0 per ACSM; 1.4–2.0 per ISSN; 1.6–2.2 per UEFA), the **same on all day types**. Varying carbohydrate by day type and keeping protein constant matches UEFA and ACSM practice.
- A modest bump (e.g. towards 1.8–2.0) on match day and MD+1 could be justified by Ranchordas (1.5–2 g/kg during congestion), but no guideline gives a separate figure, so this would be a design choice, not a cited rule.
- 1.2 g/kg is the agreed floor (ACSM); 2.0–2.2 is the upper end of routine recommendations. Above 2.2 appears only for energy deficits, which the app excludes.
- Consensus: high on "1.2–2.0", moderate on "1.6 as optimum" (statistically uncertain).

### Gaps
- No guideline gives protein g/kg per day type (match vs training vs rest). Searched ACSM 2016, UEFA 2021, ISSN 2017, Ranchordas 2017; none found.
- No futsal-specific intervention studies on protein needs. Only descriptive dietary surveys exist (Brum 2023, Gogojewicz 2023, women's futsal scoping review 2025).
- The IOC consensus (2011 and later updates) was not fetched in this session; its figures (1.3–1.8 g/kg/d in the 2011 statement, from memory) are **unverified here**.
- The primary texts of Nunes 2022 and Tagawa 2021 were not read. Their figures come from a secondary blog.

## 2. Protein distribution: dose per meal, number of meals, pre-sleep protein

### Takeaway
Consensus: **~0.3 g/kg per meal (range 0.25–0.4 g/kg, ~20–40 g)**, every **3–5 h**, across **3–4 main meals (± snacks)**, plus a pre-sleep protein serving (**~30–40 g casein**, or ~0.4 g/kg in a meal). Sources disagree slightly: UEFA says "at least ~0.4 g/kg per meal", ACSM says 0.3 g/kg, ISSN says 0.25 g/kg or 20–40 g.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM/AND/DC 2016: muscle adaptation "can be maximized by ingesting these targets as **0.3 g/kg body weight after key exercise sessions and every 3–5 hours over multiple meals**." Early recovery (0–2 h) target "~10 g essential amino acids ... translates to a recommended protein intake of **0.25–0.3 g/kg** body weight or **15–25 g** protein across the typical range of athlete body sizes"; higher doses "(ie, ≥40 g dietary protein) have not yet been shown to further augment MPS and may only be prudent for the largest athletes, or during weight loss." — [ACSM/AND/DC 2016](http://drugfreesport.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Position-stand-on-Nutrition-Athletic-Performance-ACSM-2016-1.pdf)
- ISSN 2017 (protein): acute dose "**0.25 g of a high-quality protein per kg** of body weight, or an absolute dose of **20–40 g**"; ~700–3000 mg leucine per dose; doses "evenly distributed, **every 3–4 h**, across the day"; pre-sleep "**30–40 g** of casein" increases overnight MPS and metabolic rate "without influencing lipolysis". — [Jäger et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/)
- ISSN 2017 (timing): protein "20–40 g (0.25–0.40 g/kg) every 3–4 h"; pre-sleep casein "~30–40 g". — [Kerksick et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28919842/)
- UEFA 2021: "Ideally, **three to four** discrete protein-containing meals should be consumed each day, with **at least ~0.4 g/kg BM/meal**, which at four meals would provide ~1.6 g protein/kg BM/day"; "~**2.5 g of leucine per meal** would be optimal" (e.g. 25 g whey, 140 g lean beef or chicken breast, 5 eggs, 30 g soy isolate); with supplements "0.3–0.4 g/kg BM/meal"; players typically skew intake (dinner > lunch > breakfast > snacks). — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- UEFA 2021 pre-sleep: "Preliminary evidence supports presleep protein ingestion at a dose of **~0.4 g/kg BM within 3 hours of bed** in a full meal or perhaps **0.5 g/kg BM** if consumed as supplemental protein 1–2 hours before bed"; professional players typically ate only 0.1 g/kg at that time. In the post-match section: "emerging evidence that consuming **30–60 g of casein** protein prior to sleep can enhance overnight protein synthesis." — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- Ranchordas 2017: "Meals and snacks should be divided into **6 × 20–25 g** protein servings over the day, interspersed by roughly **3 h**"; pre-bed snack "preferably contains casein (e.g. **200 g of cottage cheese** or **40 g** in a liquid supplement)". Food portions with ~10 g protein: 40 g cooked chicken or lean meat, 300 ml milk, 2 small eggs, 30 g reduced-fat cheese, 120 g tofu, 50 g canned tuna or salmon. — [Ranchordas et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5596842)
- Morton 2018 recommends "~1.6 g/kg/day, separated into **~0.25 g/kg doses**", citing a per-meal MPS breakpoint of 0.24 g/kg in young adults (0.40 g/kg in older). — [Morton et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867436/)
- Newer evidence (post-2023) against a strict per-meal ceiling: 100 g protein after resistance exercise produced "a greater and more prolonged (>12 h) anabolic response when compared to the ingestion of 25 g protein", with negligible rise in amino acid oxidation; "the magnitude and duration of the anabolic response to protein ingestion is not restricted". — [Trommelen et al. 2023, Cell Rep Med](https://www.cell.com/cell-reports-medicine/fulltext/S2666-3791(23)00540-2). This is debated (Witard & Mettler, with a reply by Trommelen, Holwerda & van Loon 2024, [IJSNEM DOI 10.1123/ijsnem.2024-0107](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.2024-0107)).
- Res et al. 2012 (40 g casein pre-sleep raised overnight protein synthesis) is reference 49 in Ranchordas 2017: "protein ingested before sleep has proven to be effectively digested and absorbed, leading to an increase in protein synthesis and improving whole-body protein balance during overnight recovery". — [Ranchordas et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5596842)

### Inferences
- Rule-engine suggestion: per-meal target = **0.3 g/kg** (bounds 0.25–0.4 g/kg; floor ~20 g); **4 eating occasions** spaced ~3–5 h; with 1.6 g/kg/day this works out as roughly 4 × 0.4 g/kg. A pre-sleep item of ~0.4 g/kg (about 30–40 g, e.g. cottage cheese, quark, Greek yoghurt or milk) fits every guideline and is food-first, which suits players without supplements.
- The code should check per-meal protein as a *minimum* (≥0.25 g/kg), not a maximum. Trommelen 2023 weakens the case for capping per-meal protein, but the daily total remains the main driver.
- Moderate–high consensus on 0.25–0.4 g/kg every 3–5 h; pre-sleep protein is labelled "emerging/preliminary" by UEFA.

### Gaps
- Snijders et al. 2015 (J Nutr; ~27.5 g pre-sleep protein over 12 weeks of training) was not fetched; figures unverified in this session.
- No futsal-specific data on meal distribution.

## 3. Post-exercise recovery: protein, timing window, carbohydrate co-ingestion, match vs training

### Takeaway
After a **match**: carbohydrate **~1–1.2 g/kg/h for ~4 h** plus **~20–40 g protein** (~0.3 g/kg) soon after, then 20–25 g every 3–4 h, pre-sleep protein, and 6–8 g/kg/day carbohydrate for 24–72 h (UEFA). Rehydrate to ~150% of fluid lost. After **training**: the same protein dose (0.3 g/kg) after key sessions, but the strict "anabolic window" is weak when daily protein is adequate. A "within ~2 h" target is a reasonable, low-cost rule. Adding protein (0.2–0.4 g/kg/h) lets carbohydrate drop to ~0.8 g/kg/h with similar glycogen resynthesis.

### Cited Findings
- UEFA 2021 post-match: "Postmatch meals and snacks should target a CHO intake of **~1 g/kg BM/hour for 4 hours**"; daily CHO "**6–8 g/kg BM in the 24 hours following a game (MD +1)** ... maintained for up to **48–72 hours** after the match during congested fixture schedules"; "glycogen synthesis is impaired in the presence of muscle damage"; protein "**20–25 g** of (high-quality) protein at **3–4 hour** intervals"; pre-sleep casein 30–60 g. — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- UEFA 2021: "in most situations there is sufficient opportunity and time to restore euhydration and electrolyte balance with normal eating/drinking practices". — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- Ranchordas 2017 (elite soccer, ~48 h between matches): "restoration of liver and muscle glycogen stores (via consumption of **~1.2 g/kg/h** of carbohydrate) and augmentation of protein synthesis (via **~40 g of protein**) should be prioritised in the **first 20 min** of recovery"; daily CHO **6–10 g/kg** between repeated matches through "3–4 main meals and regular carbohydrate snacking"; 30–40 g protein (6–9 g EAA) "as soon as possible after exercise"; fluids ≥150% (150–200%) of body mass lost (rough guide 1 kg = 1.5 L) within 2–4 h, with sodium. Players who played little "do not require the same level of energy intake". — [Ranchordas et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5596842)
- Ranchordas 2017: "consuming 40 g of protein rather than just 20 g after exercise stimulates greater myofibrillar protein synthesis irrespective of the lean body mass" (citing Macnaughton et al. 2016); "0.4 g/kg/h of protein was added to 0.8 g/kg/h of carbohydrate" gave similar glycogen synthesis to 1.2 g/kg/h CHO alone; milk/flavoured milk is a good recovery drink for lactose-tolerant players; supercompensation of glycogen 48 h after a soccer game "has yet to be reported", likely due to eccentric muscle damage. — [Ranchordas et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5596842)
- ISSN nutrient timing 2017: rapid refeeding (<4 h recovery) **1.2 g/kg/h** CHO, or **0.8 g/kg/h CHO + 0.2–0.4 g/kg/h protein**; daily CHO 8–12 g/kg for maximal glycogen; post-exercise protein "immediately to 2 h" after exercise; 20–40 g. — [Kerksick et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28919842/)
- ACSM/AND/DC 2016: MPS optimised by ~10 g EAA "in the early recovery phase (**0–2 h** after exercise)" = 0.25–0.3 g/kg; "While protein timing affects MPS rates, the magnitude of mass and strength changes over time are less clear"; exercise sensitises muscle to protein "for at least 24 hours". — [ACSM/AND/DC 2016](http://drugfreesport.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Position-stand-on-Nutrition-Athletic-Performance-ACSM-2016-1.pdf)
- Schoenfeld, Aragon & Krieger 2013 (meta-regression; strength: 20 studies, 478 subjects; hypertrophy: 23 studies, 525 subjects): a simple pooled analysis showed a small–moderate hypertrophy effect of timing, but "In the full meta-regression model controlling for all covariates, however, no significant differences were found ... total protein intake was the strongest predictor"; "if a peri-workout anabolic window ... does in fact exist, the window ... would appear to be greater than one-hour before and after". The control groups ate less protein (~1.3 vs ~1.7 g/kg). — [Schoenfeld et al. 2013](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1186/1550-2783-10-53); intake figures from [Schoenfeld et al. 2017 (pre vs post), CUNY](https://academicworks.cuny.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1219&context=le_pubs)
- Aragon & Schoenfeld 2013 frame pre- and post-exercise protein (~0.4–0.5 g/kg each, per the full text; abstract has no numbers) as "a simple, relatively fail-safe general guideline". — [Aragon & Schoenfeld 2013](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1186/1550-2783-10-5) (exact numbers not verified in this session)
- Practical, all levels: "combining both carbohydrate and protein immediately post-training is an easy strategy for players at all levels" (milk drinks, smoothies, cereal with yoghurt/milk, lean-meat sandwiches); 20–25 g protein in the recovery meal. — [Collins & Rollo 2014, GSSI SSE #133](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/sse-133-practical-considerations-in-elite-football)
- Glycogen timing urgency depends on recovery time: the aggressive 1.2 g/kg/h approach is framed by ISSN for "<4 h recovery" and by Ranchordas for ~48 h between matches. — [Kerksick et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28919842/); [Ranchordas et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5596842)

### Inferences
- Match vs training for the rule engine:
  - **Match day (post-match)**: recovery meal/snack within ~1 h: protein ~0.3–0.4 g/kg (20–40 g) + carbohydrate ~1 g/kg; continue carbohydrate-rich meals; pre-sleep protein. The next day (MD+1) is high-carbohydrate (UEFA 6–8 g/kg; the carbohydrate notes cover this).
  - **Hard training**: recovery meal within ~2 h with 0.3 g/kg protein + carbohydrate. Since amateurs rarely play two games within 48 h, the "first 20 min / 1.2 g/kg/h" urgency is mostly unnecessary if the next meal comes within ~2 h.
  - **Light training/rest**: normal protein distribution; no special recovery snack.
- Late evening games (common in amateur futsal) compress the post-match window into the pre-sleep window, so one combined recovery + pre-sleep meal (protein ~0.4 g/kg + carbohydrate) is a pragmatic rule. Ranchordas notes night games limit time and advises liquid sources (milkshakes) and no caffeine before sleep.
- Consensus: high on carbohydrate + protein after matches; low–moderate on the importance of a tight timing window (meta-analytic evidence says total daily intake dominates).

### Gaps
- No futsal-specific recovery nutrition trials found. Futsal (2 × 20 min stopped-clock, unlimited substitutions) has different total load, and playing time per player varies a lot. Football evidence is extrapolated.
- UEFA itself notes: "There is little research specific to football ... Those studies that have used football as a model have been done with players engaged at recreational level. There is very little information derived from studies of elite players." — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- Post-2023 football-specific recovery reviews were not located in this session's searches.

## 4. Fat intake: g/kg vs % energy, minimum, omega-3

### Takeaway
Guidelines give fat only as **% of energy (20–35%)**, consistent with public-health guidance, and **discourage chronic intakes <20%** (UEFA: "<15–20%"). No authoritative body gives a g/kg figure (a "1 g/kg" figure appears only in non-peer-reviewed coaching material). Saturated fat <10% of energy. Omega-3: no consensus to supplement; eat oily fish. Fish-oil doses of 1.8–3 g/day for muscle soreness have conflicting evidence. UEFA does not recommend nutraceuticals (including n-3) for inflammation or rehabilitation.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM/AND/DC 2016: "Intake of fat by athletes should be in accordance with public health guidelines and should be individualized"; saturated fat "limited to less than 10%"; "Athletes should be discouraged from chronic implementation of fat intakes **below 20% of energy intake** since the reduction in dietary variety often associated with such restrictions is likely to reduce the intake of a variety of nutrients such as fat-soluble vitamins and essential fatty acids, especially n-3 fatty acids"; short-term low fat acceptable only "in acute scenarios such as the pre-event diet or carbohydrate-loading"; high-fat low-carbohydrate diets impair high-intensity performance. Summary statement: "Consuming ≤20% of energy intake from fat does not benefit performance". — [ACSM/AND/DC 2016](http://drugfreesport.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Position-stand-on-Nutrition-Athletic-Performance-ACSM-2016-1.pdf)
- UEFA 2021: following community guidelines "typically leads to a fat intake of **20%–35%** of total dietary energy. While some players may restrict fat intake ... overrestricting fat intake to **<15%–20%** of energy often requires an unnecessary avoidance of a range of foods with valuable nutrient profiles"; essential fatty acids (linoleic + α-linolenic) typically supply ~10% of energy in sedentary people; "an LCHF diet is not recommended for footballers" (no team-sport LCHF studies exist). — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- UEFA 2021 on omega-3/nutraceuticals: "a range of 'nutraceuticals' (including phenolic compounds, curcuminoids and n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids) have been proposed ... direct evidence of their anti-inflammatory effects in humans is lacking ... inhibition of postinjury inflammation ... could be counterproductive"; omega-3 for muscle retention during rehab "require corroboration ... and therefore cannot be recommended at this time." Vegetarian diets may give lower omega-3 intakes. — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- Ranchordas 2017: "research on Omega-3 fatty acid supplements is conflicting"; studies found 1.8 g, 2.7 g and 3 g reduced DOMS, others found no benefit; "there does seem to be some evidence for using Omega-3 fatty acid supplementation in doses of **1.8 to 3 g per day** to reduce inflammation and muscle soreness after matches, especially during periods of fixture congestion" (elite context); food sources: oily fish, beans, flax seeds, walnuts; "further research is warranted in elite team sports"; antioxidant/anti-inflammatory supplements "should be carefully dosed" because adaptations are triggered by inflammatory/redox signalling. — [Ranchordas et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5596842)
- Practical football guidance: "eating oily fish three times a week, cooking using plant based oils and eating lean cuts of meat and low fat dairy products". — [Collins & Rollo 2014, GSSI](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/sse-133-practical-considerations-in-elite-football)
- Contradicting/low-quality source: a futsal coaching handout states "Fat intake 1 g/kg/day for most Athletes" — [McDonald, futsalplanet.com PDF](http://www.futsalplanet.com/files/news-healthyeatingforfutsal.pdf) (not peer-reviewed, no citation; do not use as a rule).
- Futsal intakes: Azorean amateurs 30.1% of energy from fat (83.9 g/day); top Polish players 32.7% of energy, but saturated fat above recommendations in 100% of players. — [Brum et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10055704/); [Gogojewicz et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10490003/)

### Inferences
- Rule engine: fat = **20–35% of energy**, minimum 20% as a hard floor (a safety limit that matches the project's "no aggressive restriction" rule), saturated fat <10% of energy as a quality check. Fat is the "remainder" macronutrient after carbohydrate (by day type) and protein are set. Ideally the code should flag the plan if fat goes below 20% on any day, e.g. on high-carbohydrate days.
- On match day itself (pre-match meal), lower fat for gastrointestinal comfort is acceptable acutely, per ACSM.
- Omega-3: recommend food (oily fish ~2–3×/week) rather than supplements. Supplement doses are out of scope (consistent with the "food first" stance and the no-medical-advice rule).

### Gaps
- EFSA Dietary Reference Values for fats (EFSA NDA Panel, EFSA Journal 2010;8(3):1461, DOI 10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1461: reference intake 20–35% of energy for adults; AI of 250 mg/day EPA+DHA). This is **from prior knowledge; the page was not fetched in this session**. Verify before citing in code.
- No sports body gives fat in g/kg. No futsal-specific fat or omega-3 trials were found.
- The IOC 2018 consensus on dietary supplements (Maughan et al., BJSM, DOI 10.1136/bjsports-2018-099027) was not fetched, so its stance on omega-3 is unverified here.

## 5. Applicability to amateurs vs elite; degree of consensus; contradictions

### Takeaway
The figures are physiological (g/kg) and mostly come from studies of **recreationally active or trained non-elite adults**. They transfer reasonably to healthy adult amateurs. The UEFA and Ranchordas *contexts* (congested fixtures, evening games, travel, high volumes) are elite, so the aggressive recovery tactics are less needed for amateurs who play about once a week.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM/AND/DC 2016 targets "competitive athletes" in general, but the recommendations "encompass most training regimens and allow for flexible adjustments"; experienced athletes may need less protein. — [ACSM/AND/DC 2016](http://drugfreesport.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Position-stand-on-Nutrition-Athletic-Performance-ACSM-2016-1.pdf)
- ISSN 2017: 1.4–2.0 g/kg/day for "most exercising individuals", not only elite. — [Jäger et al. 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28642676/)
- UEFA 2021 is written for elite football, but states that the football studies used "players engaged at recreational level". — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- Morton 2018: healthy adults (trained and untrained) doing resistance training; protein supplementation was "more effective in resistance-trained individuals", and the effect fell with age. — [Morton et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867436/)
- Ranchordas 2017 is explicitly about **elite** soccer with ~48 h between matches. — [Ranchordas et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5596842)
- Amateur futsal players already meet protein targets on average but miss energy and carbohydrate targets. — [Brum et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10055704/)

### Inferences — contradictions summary for the report writer
| Topic | ACSM/AND/DC 2016 | ISSN 2017 | UEFA 2021 | Ranchordas 2017 | Morton 2018 |

| Daily protein | 1.2–2.0 g/kg | 1.4–2.0 g/kg | up to 1.6–2.2 g/kg | 1.5–2.0 g/kg (congestion) | plateau 1.62 (CI 1.03–2.20) |
| Per meal | 0.3 g/kg (0.25–0.3; 15–25 g) | 0.25 g/kg or 20–40 g | ≥~0.4 g/kg; 20–25 g post-match | 20–25 g; 30–40 g post-match | ~0.25 g/kg |

| Fat | public health; not <20% chronically | — | 20–35%; avoid <15–20% | — | — |

- Key tensions: (a) per-meal 0.25 vs ≥0.4 g/kg; (b) 20 g vs 40 g post-exercise (40 g > 20 g per Macnaughton 2016; Trommelen 2023 suggests no ceiling); (c) the timing "window": tight in Ranchordas (20 min) vs weak per the Schoenfeld 2013 meta-analysis; (d) omega-3: Ranchordas cautiously positive, UEFA negative.
- Amateur applicability: daily g/kg and distribution rules are high-confidence for amateurs. Elite-congestion tactics (1.2 g/kg/h CHO in the first 20 min, 6–10 g/kg/day) are lower priority for once-a-week amateur matches.
- All figures must be labelled "pendiente de validar por un dietista-nutricionista" in the code, per the project rules.

### Gaps
- No study found that directly compares protein or recovery needs of amateur vs elite futsal or football players.
- There is very little futsal-specific evidence. Futsal research is descriptive (dietary intake surveys), and women's futsal reviews note under-consumption of energy and carbohydrate (da Silva & Barreira 2025, [DOI 10.5152/rss.2025.25057](https://doi.org/10.5152/rss.2025.25057)).
