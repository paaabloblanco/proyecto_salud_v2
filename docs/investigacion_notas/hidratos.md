## Q1. Daily carbohydrate targets by training load (ACSM/AND/DC 2016 and later updates)

### Takeaway
ACSM/AND/DC 2016, which takes its numbers from IOC 2011 (Burke et al.), gives four daily bands: light 3–5, moderate 5–7, high 6–10 and very high 8–12 g/kg/day. They are "high carbohydrate availability" targets for sessions where quality or intensity matters. I found no later ACSM/AND/DC update to the table. Football-specific guidance (UEFA 2021 and Anderson 2022) narrows the practical range to **3–8 g/kg/day**.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM 2016 Table 2, "Daily needs for fuel and recovery": **Light** (low-intensity or skill-based activities) **3–5 g/kg/day**; **Moderate** (moderate exercise program, about 1 h/day) **5–7 g/kg/day**; **High** (endurance program, 1–3 h/day of moderate-to-high-intensity exercise) **6–10 g/kg/day**; **Very high** (extreme commitment, more than 4–5 h/day of moderate-to-high intensity) **8–12 g/kg/day**. — [Thomas et al. 2016, JAND PDF Table 2](https://eclass.hmu.gr/modules/document/file.php/YD120/%CE%91%CE%A1%CE%98%CE%A1%CE%91/ACSM%20-POSITION%20PAPER%20-%20Nutrition%20and%20Athletic%20Performance%202016%20.pdf); identical in the [MSSE version](https://sky.sausport.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/American-College-of-Sports-Medicine_Joint-Position_Nutrition_and_Athletic_Performance_2016.pdf) and the [Dietitians of Canada version](https://www.dietitians.ca/DietitiansOfCanada/media/Documents/Resources/noap-position-paper.pdf)
- Footnotes to the same table: (1) the targets apply "for scenarios where it is important to exercise with high quality and/or at high intensity" and "should be fine-tuned with individual consideration of total energy needs, specific training needs and feedback from training performance"; (2) "when exercise quality or intensity is less important, it may be less important to achieve these carbohydrate targets"; intake may then "suit energy goals, food preferences, or food availability"; (3) low carbohydrate availability may be deliberately used to enhance adaptation. The table also says that "as long as total fuel needs are provided, the pattern of intake may simply be guided by convenience and individual choice", and that athletes should choose nutrient-rich carbohydrate sources. — [Thomas et al. 2016 (MSSE PDF)](https://sky.sausport.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/American-College-of-Sports-Medicine_Joint-Position_Nutrition_and_Athletic_Performance_2016.pdf)
- ACSM 2016 cites Burke et al. 2011 (IOC) as the source of the table (reference 36). — [MSSE PDF](https://sky.sausport.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/American-College-of-Sports-Medicine_Joint-Position_Nutrition_and_Athletic_Performance_2016.pdf); [Burke et al. 2011](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2011.585473)
- The position paper says athletes "should be referred to a registered dietitian nutritionist for a personalized nutrition plan". — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26920240/)
- The Australian Institute of Sport fact sheet reproduces the same bands, 3–5, 5–7, 6–10 and 8–12 g/kg. — [AIS Carbohydrate fact sheet (via RDASC)](https://www.rdasc.org.uk/Portals/0/adam/LayoutApp/3w5VA4g3vkagDTFodv3Zpg/Body/CarbohydrateAISFactsheet-1.pdf)
- ISSN 2017 says glycogen stores "are maximized by following a high-carbohydrate diet (8–12 g/kg/day)". It frames this for athletes doing at least 8 h/week of high-volume training who need to replenish glycogen continually and rapidly. — [Kerksick et al. 2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12970-017-0189-4)
- UEFA 2021 says daily CHO requirements for training "should operate on a sliding scale of 3–8 g/kg BM/day depending on the specific training scenario, fixture schedule and player-specific training goals". — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- History of the football guidelines: in 1994, 55–65% of energy from CHO and 7–10 g/kg/day before a game; in 2006, 5–7 g/kg/day on moderate training days and up to 12 g/kg/day on intense days or before a match; in 2020/21 (UEFA), 3–8 g/kg/day. — [Foo et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12152048/)

### Inferences
- A 1.5 h amateur futsal session 2–3 times a week fits the ACSM "moderate" band (5–7) at most. On non-training days it falls in the "light" band (3–5). The ACSM "high" and "very high" bands assume endurance volumes of 1–3 h or more than 4–5 h a day, which amateur futsal players do not reach.
- The UEFA scale is lower than the ACSM "high" band. For a football or futsal rule engine, UEFA's 3–8 g/kg/day is the more sport-specific frame. ACSM can serve as a cross-check.

### Gaps
- I found no updated ACSM/AND/DC position after 2016. A search for 2023–2026 joint positions turned up nothing that replaces Table 2.
- None of the bands has been validated specifically in amateur futsal players.

## Q2. Carbohydrate periodization ("fuel for the work required") and football values (UEFA 2021, Impey 2018, Anderson 2022)

### Takeaway
The current football consensus periodizes intake. Days around a match (MD-1, MD, MD+1) get **6–8 g/kg/day**. Ordinary training days in a one-match week get **3–6 g/kg/day**. The off-season gets **less than 4 g/kg/day**. Congested fixtures keep intake at 6–8 g/kg/day for the 48–72 h between games. The model comes from Impey et al. 2018, a theoretical framework built mostly on cycling and endurance data. The football figures were set from data interpretation plus practitioner experience, not from randomized trials in elite players (Foo et al. 2025).

### Cited Findings
- UEFA 2021, **MD-1**: "training is usually light and CHO intake should be at least 6–8 g/kg body mass (BM) to elevate muscle and liver glycogen stores." — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- UEFA 2021, **MD-1, MD and MD+1**: "players should increase CHO intake on MD-1, MD and MD+1 to between 6 and 8 g/kg BM. However, even at ~8 g/kg BM, muscle glycogen content in type II fibres may not be completely restored 48 hours after a match." — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- UEFA 2021, **typical training days** (one session a day in a one-match week): "daily intakes ranging from 3 to 6 g/kg BM may be sufficient to promote fuelling and recovery"; "it is unlikely that most players require CHO intake during training". — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- UEFA 2021 Table 1, "The training carbohydrate intake continuum" — [Collins et al. 2021 PDF](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/55/8/416.full.pdf):
  - **Preseason** (60–180 min, 3–12 km, HSR over 400 m): **4–8 g/kg/day**. Twice-a-day sessions about 6–8; reduced load about 4–6.
  - **In-season, one game a week** (45–90 min, 2–7 km, HSR 0–400 m): **3–8 g/kg/day**. MD-1 and MD+1 6–8; other days 3–6, "depending on training intensity, duration and player-specific goals".
  - **In-season, congested fixtures** (less than 60 min, less than 3 km, HSR under 50 m): **6–8 g/kg/day**, kept "in the 48–72 hours period between games".
  - **Off-season**: **less than 4 g/kg/day**, "to avoid gains in fat mass", and higher if the player keeps training.
- UEFA 2021 reports actual intakes in English Premier League players: about **4 g/kg on training days and 6 g/kg on match days**, and "daily intake may be closer to about 4 g/kg BM" between games. — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- UEFA 2021 is written for elite players: "The key recommendations are aimed at both male and female professional players, the majority of whom will be training and playing full time." The same paper concedes that "Those studies that have used football as a model have been done with players engaged at recreational level. There is very little information derived from studies of elite players." — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- Impey 2018 defines "fuel for the work required" as a model in which "CHO availability is adjusted in accordance with the demands of the upcoming training session(s)". It proposes a glycogen-threshold window of about **300–100 mmol/kg dry weight** for "train-low" adaptations, and says that starting exercise below **200 mmol/kg dw** is likely to impair training intensity. It also warns that repeated daily training with low CHO "may increase susceptibility to illness". The worked example is road cycling, and the authors acknowledge that the glycogen cost of habitual sessions is "not well known". — [Impey et al. 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-018-0867-7)
- Anderson 2022 gives a worked one-match-week model: about **4 g/kg on training days**, rising to **8 g/kg on MD-1, MD and MD+1**. For two-match weeks it suggests a consistently high 6–8 g/kg, with possibly 3–5 g/kg on MD+2/MD-2 for players with specific goals or non-starters. For three-match weeks it suggests 8 g/kg. It states that **"CHO periodization should not be practiced by youth or female players"** because of the risk of low energy availability. — [Anderson et al. 2022](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2022.2044135)
- Foo 2025: the guidelines "largely rely on data interpretation and practitioner experience" because randomized trials on elite players are lacking. Only 8 experimental studies were exclusively on professional players. Experimental studies mostly used recreationally active (about 40%) or collegiate (about 26%) participants, and about 75% were laboratory-based. Female players made up about 16% of participants. — [Foo et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12152048/)
- Women's football: average in-season intakes of elite female players are about 3–5.5 g/kg/day, and they typically do not periodize. — [GSSI SSE #227](https://www.gssiweb.org/docs/default-source/sse-docs/sse_227_005_final.pdf?sfvrsn=2). This is a secondary industry-funded source, but it cites peer-reviewed studies.

### Inferences
- A rule engine could map day types as follows: match day → 6–8; MD-1 → 6–8; MD+1, if another match follows within 72 h → 6–8; hard training → toward the top of 3–6, or up to about 6; light training → bottom of 3–6; rest → about 3–5 (UEFA "lower" days, ACSM "light"). For "rest" in-season, UEFA gives no separate number. Its off-season figure (less than 4) belongs to the off-season, not to an in-season rest day.
- Given Anderson 2022's warning, and the app's safety rule against restrictive diets, deliberate "train-low" strategies should be left out. The app should use only the "fuel up when needed" side of periodization.

### Gaps
- UEFA gives no explicit separate figure for an in-season rest day.
- No study has validated football periodization models in amateurs who train 2–3 times a week.

## Q3. Pre-match carbohydrate loading for intermittent sports lasting less than 90 min: 10–12 g/kg, or 6–8 g/kg on MD-1?

### Takeaway
The sources agree. ACSM/IOC reserve full **carbohydrate loading (10–12 g/kg/day for 36–48 h)** for events lasting **more than 90 min** of sustained or intermittent exercise. For events under 90 min, they recommend "general fuelling up" at **7–12 g/kg per 24 h**. Football-specific guidance (UEFA 2021, Anderson 2022, Pueyo 2024) recommends **at least 6–8 g/kg on MD-1**, not 10–12. Futsal is shorter (2×20 min of stopped-clock time) but more intense. Full loading is therefore not justified, and **6–8 g/kg on MD-1** is the defensible football-derived value.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM 2016 Table 2, "Acute fueling strategies": **General fueling up**, "preparation for events <90 min exercise", **7–12 g/kg per 24 h as for daily fuel needs**. **Carbohydrate loading**, "preparation for events >90 min of sustained/intermittent exercise", **36–48 h of 10–12 g/kg BM per 24 h**. Athletes "may choose carbohydrate-rich sources that are low in fiber/residue and easily consumed" for gut comfort. — [Thomas et al. 2016 JAND PDF](https://eclass.hmu.gr/modules/document/file.php/YD120/%CE%91%CE%A1%CE%98%CE%A1%CE%91/ACSM%20-POSITION%20PAPER%20-%20Nutrition%20and%20Athletic%20Performance%202016%20.pdf); [AIS fact sheet](https://www.rdasc.org.uk/Portals/0/adam/LayoutApp/3w5VA4g3vkagDTFodv3Zpg/Body/CarbohydrateAISFactsheet-1.pdf)
- UEFA 2021: on MD-1, "CHO intake should be at least 6–8 g/kg". — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- Anderson 2022: professional players "are likely to achieve high glycogen stores with 24 h of a CHO rich diet (Bussau et al., 2002) providing that training intensity and duration is significantly reduced on MD-1"; daily intakes "should equate to at least 6–8 g/kg". EPL players report only about **4 g/kg on MD-1**, which may reduce total distance compared with 8 g/kg (Souglis et al. 2013). — [Anderson et al. 2022](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2022.2044135)
- Pueyo 2024 (systematic review, 61 studies, 1111 adult players) recommends **6–8 g/kg/day the day before**. Studies it reviews: a diet of 8 g/kg/day for 3–4 days allowed 17% more distance, at higher speeds, than 3 g/kg/day; a 7-day protocol rising to 7.5 g/kg on MD improved running variables against a 5–6 g/kg control (Kazemi et al.). — [Pueyo et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11547566/)
- Glycogen depletion in a match (football, lower-division Danish players): about 50% of muscle fibres are empty or partly empty after the match. Players starting with low glycogen cover less distance and much less at high speed, especially in the second half. — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416). Saltin 1973 (n=9): low-glycogen players covered 9,700 m against 12,000 m, and less in the second half. — [Foo et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12152048/)
- Futsal format: 2×20 min of stopped-clock time. "The game usually lasts 70–85% longer than the scheduled total of 40-min." The work-to-rest ratio is about 1:1. Professionals cover 13.7% of distance at speeds of 15 km/h or more and 8.9% sprinting (25 km/h or more), about 117 m/min, with rolling unlimited substitutions. Individual distance varies widely (601–8,040 m) depending on minutes played. — [Naser, Ali, Macadam 2017, J Exerc Sci Fit 15(2):76–80, DOI 10.1016/j.jesf.2017.09.001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5812879/)
- Futsal glycogen: Krustrup et al. (cited by Naser 2017) showed selective depletion of glycogen and creatine phosphate in fast-twitch fibres after Yo-Yo IR tests in futsal players. That was a test, not a match. — [Naser et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5812879/)

### Inferences
- Real duration is about 70–74 min (40 × 1.70–1.85). The original question estimated around 70–80 min. Rolling substitutions also mean each player is on court for less than the whole match. Futsal clearly falls in the ACSM "<90 min" category, so 10–12 g/kg loading is not indicated.
- The ACSM "<90 min" figure (7–12) is higher than the UEFA MD-1 figure (6–8). This is a contradiction between sources. For amateurs, UEFA's 6–8 is more conservative and more sport-specific, and its lower end overlaps with ACSM.

### Gaps
- No futsal study has measured muscle glycogen depletion during a real match. I found no futsal-specific trial of carbohydrate loading.
- The 6–8 g/kg MD-1 figure for football comes from expert consensus. Direct evidence comparing 6–8 with 10–12 g/kg in short intermittent sports is sparse.

## Q4. Pre-match meal (timing, g/kg) and snack 30–60 min before

### Takeaway
ACSM: **1–4 g/kg, 1–4 h before** exercise lasting more than 60 min. UEFA (football): a CHO-rich meal of **1–3 g/kg, 3–4 h before kick-off**, inside a daily total of 6–8 g/kg on MD. Pueyo 2024 agrees (1–3 g/kg, 3–4 h). For the 30–60 min pre-match snack there is no g/kg figure in the consensus documents. UEFA instead recommends about **30–60 g CHO after the warm-up**.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM 2016 / AIS, **pre-event fuelling**, "before exercise > 60 min": **1–4 g/kg consumed 1–4 h pre-competition**. — [AIS fact sheet reproducing ACSM/IOC table](https://www.rdasc.org.uk/Portals/0/adam/LayoutApp/3w5VA4g3vkagDTFodv3Zpg/Body/CarbohydrateAISFactsheet-1.pdf); [Thomas et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26920240/)
- UEFA 2021: "Within an overall guideline of 6–8 g/kg BM CHO per day, it is recommended that players consume a CHO-rich meal (1–3 g/kg BM) 3–4 hours before kick-off." Liver glycogen "can be reduced by about 50% after an overnight fast", which matters especially for lunchtime kick-offs. The meal should be easily digestible, and comfort should be considered "rather than rigid strategies focused solely on meeting CHO intake guidelines". Fluid: 5–7 mL/kg in the 2–4 h before kick-off. — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- UEFA 2021: professional youth players dribbled faster after a larger breakfast (500 vs 250 kcal, 60% CHO) eaten 135 min before. — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- Pueyo 2024 recommends "a meal with 1–3 g/kg 3–4 h before". In women, a CHO-rich pre-match meal (203 g) did not change distance or sprints compared with a mixed meal (103 g CHO) of the same calories, eaten 4 h before. — [Pueyo et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11547566/)
- ISSN 2017: "In the hours leading up to competition, glycogen levels are best maintained or increased by consuming high carbohydrate (1–4 g/kg) meals or snacks for several hours before". The position stand writes this as "1–4 g/kg/day", which appears to be a wording slip, since the context is hours, not days. — [Kerksick et al. 2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12970-017-0189-4)
- Pre-warm-up and warm-up feeding: Anderson 2022 suggests 20–30 g at the start and 20–30 g at the end of the warm-up, and says this regimen "has not been experimentally tested". — [Anderson et al. 2022](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2022.2044135)

### Inferences
- A rule could be: main pre-match meal 1–3 g/kg, 3–4 h before (UEFA), with ACSM's 1–4 g/kg over 1–4 h as the outer limit. For a small meal closer to the match (1–2 h), use the low end. No g/kg target for the 30–60 min snack can be cited from the consensus papers. Present it as an optional small, low-fibre CHO snack of about 20–30 g (Anderson 2022), not as a g/kg rule.

### Gaps
- No consensus g/kg figure for a snack 30–60 min before. No futsal-specific pre-match meal studies.

## Q5. Carbohydrate during the match and at half-time (g/h); does it apply to futsal?

### Takeaway
For football, about **30–60 g/h** is recommended, delivered as about 30–60 g after the warm-up and again at half-time (UEFA). For exercise of **45–75 min** at sustained high intensity, ACSM/IOC say only "small amounts including mouth rinse". For exercise under 45 min it is "not required". Futsal sits on the boundary (about 70 min of real time, with rotating players). In a one-off amateur match, in-game carbohydrate is optional. The evidence for performance benefits, sprinting especially, is mixed.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM 2016 / IOC 2011 table: during brief exercise (**<45 min**): **not required**. During sustained high-intensity exercise (**45–75 min**): **small amounts including mouth rinse**. During endurance exercise including **"stop and start" sports (1–2.5 h)**: **30–60 g/h**. — [AIS fact sheet (ACSM/IOC table)](https://www.rdasc.org.uk/Portals/0/adam/LayoutApp/3w5VA4g3vkagDTFodv3Zpg/Body/CarbohydrateAISFactsheet-1.pdf)
- IOC 2011: "During sustained high-intensity sports lasting ~1 h, small amounts of carbohydrate, including even mouth-rinsing, enhance performance via central nervous system effects. While 30–60 g·h⁻¹ is an appropriate target for sports of longer duration, events >2.5 h may benefit from higher intakes of up to 90 g·h⁻¹." — [Burke et al. 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21660838)
- UEFA 2021: benefits are seen in football simulations at about 30–60 g/h, "or when 60 g is consumed before each half"; it recommends "~30–60 g CHO is consumed after warm-up and again at half-time". Effects on shooting, dribbling and passing are positive. Effects on sprinting, jumping, change of direction and cognition are "less consistent". EPL players average 32 g/h. — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- Anderson 2022 suggests 20–40 g at half-time and 20–30 g in the second half if possible, for 60–120 g per game in total. — [Anderson et al. 2022](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2022.2044135)
- Baker et al. 2015 (Nutrients; Gatorade Sports Science Institute authors) report that "10 out of 12 studies have found no significant benefit of carbohydrate ingestion on sprint performance in soccer, rugby and field hockey". — [Baker, Rollo, Stein, Jeukendrup 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4517026/)
- Peeters et al. 2025 (*Performance Nutrition*, RCT, 12 regionally competitive players, a 120-min simulation after a standardized 6 g/kg the day before and 2 g/kg 2–3 h before): about 54 g/h and about 82 g/h gave no physical or cognitive benefit over placebo. — [Peeters et al. 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s44410-025-00015-5)
- Pueyo 2024: 30–60 g/h during the match improves sprint number and speed, shooting and time to fatigue in some studies. Blood glucose falls by up to 30% in the first 15 min of the second half even with CHO intake. — [Pueyo et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11547566/)
- Rodriguez-Giustiniani et al. 2019 (IJSNEM, DOI 10.1123/ijsnem.2018-0214): 60 g of CHO (12% solution, 250 mL) before each half preserved passing accuracy and improved high-intensity running capacity by 13% in elite academy players. — [Rodriguez-Giustiniani et al.](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.2018-0214)
- Futsal: Aghili 2022 (*Int J Sport Stud Health*, n=40 professional futsal players, quasi-experimental) found that a CHO drink improved reaction time and coordination. This is a small, low-impact journal. — [Aghili 2022](https://journals.kmanpub.com/index.php/Intjssh/article/view/2154)
- A practitioner guide (a New Zealand registered dietitian, non-peer-reviewed) says fuelling "isn't essential during one-off games", with 30–60 g helpful in tournaments or with several matches a day. — [Fuel Up Nutrition 2026](https://fuelupnutrition.co.nz/the-complete-guide-to-futsal-nutrition-what-players-should-eat-before-during-and-after-games/)

### Inferences
- For futsal, about 70 min of real time with rotating players, the ACSM "45–75 min → small amounts/mouth rinse" category fits better than the football 30–60 g/h. A reasonable rule: in-match CHO is optional. Offer a small amount (for example a sports drink or fruit) at half-time, and suggest 30–60 g/h only for tournaments or several matches in a day.

### Gaps
- No robust futsal-specific RCT on in-game CHO was found. The Aghili 2022 study is weak.

## Q6. Post-exercise carbohydrate for glycogen recovery: when 1.0–1.2 g/kg/h matters

### Takeaway
**1–1.2 g/kg/h for the first 4 h** is the ACSM/IOC "speedy refuelling" guideline. It applies when there are **less than 8 h** between fuel-demanding sessions. ISSN uses 1.2 g/kg/h when there are less than 4 h. UEFA recommends about **1 g/kg/h for 4 h** after a match. When the next demanding session is more than 24 h away, total daily intake matters more than timing.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM 2016 Table 2, **speedy refuelling**, "<8 h recovery between 2 fuel-demanding sessions": **1–1.2 g/kg/h for first 4 h, then resume daily fuel needs**; "there may be benefits in consuming small regular snacks". — [Thomas et al. 2016 JAND PDF](https://eclass.hmu.gr/modules/document/file.php/YD120/%CE%91%CE%A1%CE%98%CE%A1%CE%91/ACSM%20-POSITION%20PAPER%20-%20Nutrition%20and%20Athletic%20Performance%202016%20.pdf)
- ISSN 2017: if rapid restoration is needed (**<4 h of recovery**): aggressive refeeding at **1.2 g/kg/h**, preferring high-GI (>70) sources; adding caffeine (3–8 mg/kg); or combining **0.8 g/kg/h CHO with 0.2–0.4 g/kg/h protein**. Timing matters most "1) when rapid restoration of muscle glycogen is a primary goal and 2) when inadequate amounts of carbohydrate are being delivered". Ingesting ≥1.2 g/kg/h for 4–6 h after exhausting exercise rapidly replenishes glycogen. Adding protein raises resynthesis when CHO is below 1.2 g/kg/h. — [Kerksick et al. 2017](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12970-017-0189-4)
- A review of supplement timing and dose: "For an active person, who has more than 24 h of interval for the next training, glycogen can be replaced during the day and post exercise CHO intake could be ingested in lower amounts." — [PMC5545206](https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5545206/). Authors and journal were not captured in the fetch, so verify before citing.
- UEFA 2021: "Postmatch meals and snacks should target a CHO intake of ~1 g/kg BM/hour for 4 hours." MD+1: 6–8 g/kg, kept for up to 48–72 h with congested fixtures. Glycogen synthesis is impaired by muscle damage. Alcohol impairs glycogen resynthesis; up to 2 units/day occasionally is "not harmful", but players should minimise it during key periods. — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- Pueyo 2024: "1–1.5 g/kg/h is recommended during the first 4 h, starting from the first 20 min". Glycogen "drops drastically at the end of the match, remaining low at 48 h". Nielsen et al.: muscle glycogen recovered 59% the next day and a further 19% from day 2 to day 5. Gunnarsson et al.: 50% of fibres were still depleted at 48 h despite more than 8 g/kg/day. — [Pueyo et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11547566/)
- Pueyo's upper figure (1.5 g/kg/h) is higher than ACSM/UEFA (1–1.2). This is a minor contradiction.
- Anderson 2022: 6–9 g/kg/day documented glycogen resynthesis within 2–3 days after a match in Danish players. Type II fibres were still not fully restored at 48 h. — [Anderson et al. 2022](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2022.2044135)

### Inferences
- For amateur futsal (usually one match a week, training every 48–72 h), the aggressive 1–1.2 g/kg/h protocol rarely applies. It is relevant for tournaments or two sessions in a day (less than 8 h apart), or a match the next day. Otherwise a normal CHO-rich meal after the match, plus the daily target, is enough. A rule could be: if the next demanding session is less than 8 h away, apply 1–1.2 g/kg/h × 4 h; if it is less than 24–48 h away, keep 6–8 g/kg/day; otherwise use the normal day target.

### Gaps
- No futsal-specific glycogen recovery data.

## Q7. Futsal-specific evidence on carbohydrate intake or glycogen

### Takeaway
Futsal evidence is almost entirely **observational dietary surveys**. They consistently show intakes below recommendations (about 3.4–4.1 g/kg/day). There are no consensus guidelines specific to futsal, and no studies of glycogen during a match. Recommendations are extrapolated from football.

### Cited Findings
- Polish top-division futsal players (n=11, 26 ± 3.6 y; Nutrients 2023;15(17):3720): CHO **4.1 ± 1.24 g/kg/day** (range 2.8–7.2); 45% ate less than 4 g/kg; 47.7% of energy came from CHO. Energy availability was 25.7 kcal/kg FFM, against a reference of more than 45. — [MDPI Nutrients 2023](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/15/17/3720)
- Azores II-division senior male futsal players (amateur/semi-pro, n=20 with dietary recall; Brum, Franchini, Moura 2023, PMC10055704): CHO **3.4 ± 1.1 g/kg/day** (2.3–6.9), 41.7% of energy, below the ISSN 5–8 g/kg reference; protein 1.9 g/kg (adequate). — [Brum et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10055704/)
- A Brazilian futsal team (n=13, mean age 21; Revista Brasileira de Futsal e Futebol 2018): all 13 athletes ate less CHO than recommended (search-result summary only, full text not verified). — [RBFF via DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/a974b0288f8b474d9b5a0116ef3f1e2c)
- Scoping review of women's futsal (da Silva & Barreira 2025, DOI 10.5152/rss.2025.25057): female players "often fail to meet recommended levels of energy and macronutrient intake"; glycolysis is the predominant pathway. — [da Silva & Barreira 2025](https://doi.org/10.5152/rss.2025.25057)
- Futsal demands and the selective depletion of type II fibre glycogen after Yo-Yo IR tests (Krustrup et al.). — [Naser et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5812879/)
- Caffeine (not CHO) at 3 mg/kg improved sprint, jump and high-speed running in a simulated futsal match (2×7.5 min) in sub-elite players. This is an example of futsal-specific ergogenic research, but it is not carbohydrate research. — [Eur J Nutr 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00394-021-02617-w)

### Inferences
- The main practical problem for amateur futsal players is probably under-eating carbohydrate (about 3.4–4 g/kg) on match and pre-match days, not eating too much. The app should therefore nudge intake up on MD-1 and MD.

### Gaps
- I found no futsal-specific CHO position statement, no match-glycogen study, and no futsal RCT of CHO loading or pre-match meals.
- The Portuguese study mentioned in the search summary (title refers to female players, text describes 107 players) could not be checked and was excluded.

## Q8. Do these figures apply to amateurs or only to elite players? Degree of consensus

### Takeaway
UEFA's figures are explicitly **aimed at elite, full-time professionals**, yet most of the experimental evidence comes from **recreational or collegiate players** in laboratory simulations. The ACSM/IOC figures are general "athlete" guidelines scaled by training load, so they can be applied to amateurs by choosing the appropriate (lower) load band. Consensus is **high** on the structure: periodize to load, at least 6–8 on MD-1, 1–3 g/kg 3–4 h pre-match, 30–60 g/h for 90-min football, about 1 g/kg/h post-match when recovery is short. Consensus is **lower** on exact numbers and on in-game benefits for sprinting.

### Cited Findings
- UEFA recommendations are "aimed at both male and female professional players, the majority of whom will be training and playing full time". — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- UEFA limitations: "Those studies that have used football as a model have been done with players engaged at recreational level. There is very little information derived from studies of elite players." "Results need to be extrapolated from different sports and simpler exercise protocols." — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- Foo 2025: experimental CHO studies in football involved recreationally active (about 40%) and collegiate (about 26%) participants; only 8 were exclusively on professionals; about 75% were laboratory-based; the guidelines rely on "data interpretation and practitioner experience". — [Foo et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12152048/)
- Pueyo 2024: of 27 clinical trials on physical/technical outcomes, only 4 were on professionals. Risk of bias (RoB 2): crossover trials 24% low risk, 76% some concerns; parallel trials 33% high risk, 67% some concerns. — [Pueyo et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11547566/)
- ACSM 2016 frames its targets by load, not by competitive level, and says they should be "fine-tuned with individual consideration of total energy needs, specific training needs and feedback". — [Thomas et al. 2016](https://sky.sausport.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/American-College-of-Sports-Medicine_Joint-Position_Nutrition_and_Athletic_Performance_2016.pdf)
- Anderson 2022: periodization (especially low-CHO days) "should not be practiced by youth or female players" because of the low energy availability risk. — [Anderson et al. 2022](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2022.2044135)

### Inferences
- For amateurs who train 2–3 times a week, the lower half of each band is probably adequate on training and rest days. The match-related values (6–8 on MD-1/MD) are probably still appropriate, because the glycogen demand of the match itself does not depend much on competitive level. This last point is an inference, not a cited finding.

### Gaps
- No study validates these figures in amateur futsal players.
- Evidence on sex differences is limited (about 16% of football CHO research participants are female).
- No evidence is available on whether using the lower end of the ranges harms amateur performance.
