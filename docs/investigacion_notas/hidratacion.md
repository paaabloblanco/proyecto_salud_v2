# Hydration for intermittent indoor team sports (focus: futsal) — research notes

Unit conversions used throughout (my arithmetic, not from sources): 1 mmol Na = 23 mg Na; 1 meq Na = 1 mmol. So 20–30 mmol/L = 460–690 mg Na/L; 40 mmol/L = 920 mg/L; 50 mmol/L = 1150 mg/L. 1 kg body-mass lost during exercise is treated as 1 L sweat (standard assumption in all sources below).

## 1. Pre-exercise hydration (5–10 mL/kg in 2–4 h; any update?)

### Takeaway
All major statements converge on ~5–10 mL/kg body mass 2–4 h before exercise (UEFA 2021 narrows to 5–7 mL/kg for football), aiming for pale-yellow urine and leaving time to void the excess. No source found that changes these figures after 2016–2021. Consensus: high, but evidence grade is expert-opinion level; applies to everyone (not elite-specific).

### Cited Findings
- ACSM/AND/DC 2016: "consuming a fluid volume equivalent to 5–10 ml/kg BW (~2–4 ml/lb) in the 2 to 4 hours before exercise to achieve urine that is pale yellow in color while allowing for sufficient time for excess fluid to be voided." Sodium in pre-exercise foods/fluids "may help with fluid retention." — [Thomas et al. 2016, DOI 10.1249/MSS.0000000000000852](https://www.dietitians.ca/DietitiansOfCanada/media/Documents/Resources/noap-position-paper.pdf)
- ACSM 2007 (Sawka): ~5–7 mL/kg at least 4 h before; if no urine or urine is dark, a further ~3–5 mL/kg about 2 h before. — figures as quoted by [Ma et al. 2025, Sci Rep 15:43899](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12708657/) citing ACSM; the ACSM abstract confirms prehydrating "should be initiated when needed at least several hours before the activity to enable fluid absorption and allow urine output to return to normal levels" — [Sawka et al. 2007, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17277604/). (I did not see the 5–7 / 3–5 mL/kg sentence in the primary text myself — verify against the PDF.)
- UEFA 2021 (elite football): "players should aim to start the match euhydrated by ingesting 5–7 mL/kg BM of fluid in the 2–4 hours prior to kick-off… targeting a urine that is pale yellow in colour." Also: "For games with an early kick-off, the day prior to the match represents a key opportunity to optimise the players' hydration status." — [Collins et al. 2021, DOI 10.1136/bjsports-2019-101961](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- NATA 2017: promote euhydration (+1% to −1% of baseline body mass) before exercise (SORT A). Hyperhydration "confers no physiological or performance advantages and is not recommended" (SORT A). — [McDermott et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5634236/)
- NATA 2017: "A majority (more than 50%) of athletes in professional sports, collegiate athletics, and high school and youth sports arrive at workouts hypohydrated." — [McDermott et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5634236/)
- Recent confirmation in team sport: in collegiate (amateur-level) male soccer players, the proportion arriving hypohydrated (USG ≥1.020) was 58–82% across conditions (81.8% hot games, 58.1% cool games, 73.1% hot practice, 66.1% cool practice). — [Ma et al. 2025, Sci Rep 15:43899](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12708657/)
- Caffeine: NATA says do not discourage ~3 mg/kg caffeine before exercise on diuretic grounds (SORT A); ACSM/AND/DC 2016: warnings "appear to be overstated when it is habitually consumed in moderate (e.g., <180 mg) amounts." — [McDermott 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5634236/); [Thomas 2016](https://www.dietitians.ca/DietitiansOfCanada/media/Documents/Resources/noap-position-paper.pdf)

### Inferences
- App rule candidate (match day and hard training): "drink 5–7 mL/kg 2–4 h before" (e.g., 70 kg → ~350–490 mL), using UEFA's narrower range as the conservative choice inside ACSM's 5–10 mL/kg. For a 20:00 match that means a glass or two of water with the pre-match meal, not litres.
- Day before match: no numeric target exists in any source; UEFA only says it's a "key opportunity". The app can phrase it as "normal intake, check pale urine", not a number.
- Since >50% of athletes start hypohydrated, a pre-session drink reminder is justified for all training-day types.

### Gaps
- No source gives a separate pre-exercise volume for light vs hard training; the 5–10 mL/kg is generic.
- Exact Sawka 2007 wording for 5–7/3–5 mL/kg verified only via secondary citation.

## 2. During exercise: <2% body-mass loss, typical rates, breaks/substitutions, drinking to thirst

### Takeaway
The standard target is to limit body-mass loss to <2% (UEFA: <2–3%) while never gaining weight. For sessions under ~60–90 min in cool/temperate conditions, drinking to thirst/ad libitum is considered sufficient by most sources; planned drinking is advised for >90 min, heat, high sweat rates, or when performance matters. Futsal's unlimited rolling substitutions give natural drinking opportunities, and elite futsal data show players stay ~1% dehydrated drinking ad libitum. Real contradiction exists between "drink to thirst" and "planned drinking" camps.

### Cited Findings
**Thresholds**
- ACSM 2007: goal of drinking during exercise "is to prevent excessive (>2% body weight loss from water deficit) dehydration and excessive changes in electrolyte balance." "Individuals should periodically drink (as opportunities allow) during exercise, if it is expected they will become excessively dehydrated." — [Sawka et al. 2007](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/717055_6)
- ACSM/AND/DC 2016: deficits >2% BW "can compromise cognitive function and aerobic exercise performances, particularly in hot weather"; decrements in anaerobic/high-intensity, sport-specific technical skills and aerobic exercise in cool environments "are more commonly seen when 3%–5% of BW is lost." — [Thomas et al. 2016](http://drugfreesport.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Position-stand-on-Nutrition-Athletic-Performance-ACSM-2016-1.pdf)
- NATA 2017: body-mass loss "of less than 2% at the end of exercise" (SORT A); thermoregulation compromised >1%, heat-illness risk rises >3% (SORT B); individuals "should not gain body mass… during exercise" (SORT A); "should not consume fluid that exceeds their exercise-related body mass losses" (SORT A). Athletic perspective: mild–moderate hypohydration 2–5%, severe >5%. Core temperature rises ~0.15–0.20 °C and HR 3–5 bpm per 1% body-mass lost. — [McDermott et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5634236/)
- UEFA 2021 (elite football): "players should aim to drink sufficient fluids to prevent a deficit of >2%–3% of pre-exercise BM during exercise, while avoiding gains in BM (hyperhydration)." Notes individual players "may be more or less sensitive to hypohydration." — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)

**Typical rates**
- ACSM 2007: predicted sweat rates ~0.4 to ~1.8 L/h (running models); suggested marathon starting point 0.4–0.8 L/h ad libitum; "inappropriate to use a single fluid replacement rate for all." — [Sawka et al. 2007](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/717055_6)
- UEFA 2021: male football players' sweat rates 0.5–2.5 L/h in training and matches; lower in female players. — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- NATA 2017: with adequate access and thirst-driven drinking, "humans replace roughly two-thirds of sweat losses." Kenefick 2018: ad libitum replaces "about half of fluid losses." — [McDermott 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5634236/); [Kenefick 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5790864/)

**Thirst vs planned drinking (contradiction)**
- Kenefick 2018: drinking to thirst "may be sufficient" for "short duration exercise of < 1 h to 90 min; exercise in cooler conditions; and lower-intensity exercise." Planned drinking optimal for ">90 min, particularly in the heat; higher-intensity exercise with high sweat rates; exercise where performance is a concern; and when carbohydrate intake of 1 g/min is desired." "It is recommended to never drink so much that weight is gained." — [Kenefick 2018, DOI 10.1007/s40279-017-0844-6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5790864/)
- Goulet & Hoffman 2019 meta-analysis (7 studies, 82 subjects, cycling/running 1–2 h, ~28 °C): ad libitum (505 mL/h, −2.1% BM) vs programmed (1073 mL/h, −1.0% BM) gave similar endurance performance (ALD +0.98%, "likely trivial"). — [Goulet & Hoffman 2019, Sports Med, PubMed 30659500](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30659500/) (DOI not captured; likely 10.1007/s40279-018-01051-z — verify)
- Goulet 2012 meta-analysis: dehydration ≤4% BW "very unlikely to impair" endurance performance in time-trial (real-world) protocols; the 2% rule derives from fixed-intensity protocols; recommends drinking to thirst. — [Goulet 2012, Br J Sports Med, DOI 10.1136/bjsports-2012-090958](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2012-090958)
- Counterpoint for team sports — UEFA 2021: "It has become popular… to suggest that the only advice… is to drink according to the dictates of thirst. This may not be appropriate in many team sport contexts, including football… The availability of fluids and the sensation of thirst may not coincide." Recommends individualised plans based on known sweat losses. — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- ACSM/AND/DC 2016: thirst "is usually a good indication of the need to drink but not that the athlete is dehydrated." — [Thomas et al. 2016](http://drugfreesport.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Position-stand-on-Nutrition-Athletic-Performance-ACSM-2016-1.pdf)
- Note: the endurance meta-analyses are on cycling/running, not intermittent team sport; their applicability to futsal skill/sprint performance is untested.

**Futsal: substitutions and breaks**
- Elite Spanish futsal (ElPozo Murcia, LNFS, 9 field players, 6 official matches, 22–26 °C, 32–42% RH): sweat rate 43.83 ± 14.70 mL/min (≈2.6 L/h of playing time — my conversion), fluid intake 1635 ± 785 mL/match, body-mass loss 0.99 ± 1.12%; significant correlation between playing time and dehydration (rho 0.413). Conclusion: "hydration status can be well maintained with regular substitutions and correct fluid intake." — [García-Jiménez et al., Archivos de Medicina del Deporte (abstract)](https://archivosdemedicinadeldeporte.com/articulos/upload/Abstract_sudoracion_457_140.pdf) (year/DOI not captured)
- Same group, JHSE 2015 (12 elite players): after 51.0 ± 6.2 min of game time, body mass loss 0.82 ± 0.8 kg, urine 0.21 L, sweat loss 2.29 ± 0.73 L; ad libitum intake replaced 68.4 ± 32.4% of losses; final dehydration 1.04 ± 1.06% (range −1.31 to 3.02%). — [García-Jiménez, Yuste, García-Pellicer, Hellín Martínez 2015, J Hum Sport Exerc 10(4), DOI 10.14198/jhse.2015.104.05](https://doi.org/10.14198/jhse.2015.104.05)
- Same group, 2011 (8 players, 6 games): fluid intake 1.6 ± 0.7 L, sweat loss 2.2 ± 0.8 L, 71.0 ± 33.3% replaced, dehydration 0.9 ± 1.1%. — [García-Jiménez et al. 2011, Jpn J Phys Fitness Sports Med 60(3):311–318](https://scispace.com/pdf/hydration-habits-in-elite-futsal-players-during-official-4xdbche5yx.pdf)
- By position (3 matches): forwards sweat 2458 ± 671 mL, intake 1700 mL, replaced 63.6%, deficit 1.23%; defenders sweat 1696 mL, replaced 79.5%, deficit 0.59%. — [García-Jiménez et al. 2014, Am J Sports Sci Med 2(3), DOI 10.12691/ajssm-2-3-4](https://doi.org/10.12691/ajssm-2-3-4)
- Goalkeepers vs field: GK sweat 2195 mL, intake 1205 mL, replaced 56.6%, deficit 1.27%; field players 2450 mL, 1650 mL, 71.1%, 1.00%. — [García-Jiménez et al., RICYDE (year not captured)](https://www.cafyd.com/REVISTA/ojs/index.php/ricyde/article/view/290)
- Professional Brazilian futsal training (37 technical-tactical sessions, 8 weeks): sweat 1.62 ± 0.25 L/session, sweat rate 1.13 ± 0.53 L/h, intake 1.32 L (1.02 L water + 0.31 L 6% CHO drink), body-mass loss 0.52 ± 0.19%; only one player ever lost 2–3%, none >3%. — [Wilke et al. 2016, J Strength Cond Res (NSCA JSCR, Aug 2016)](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2016/08000/metabolic_demand_and_internal_training_load_in.32.aspx) (author list/DOI from memory: Wilke CF et al., likely 10.1519/JSC.0000000000001331 — verify); thesis version [Wilke 2013](http://hdl.handle.net/1843/BUBD-9GPPWC)

### Inferences
- For futsal sessions ≤90 min in a temperate pavilion, "drink to thirst at every break/substitution, with a bottle always at the bench" is consistent with Kenefick 2018 and with the futsal data (ad libitum → ~1% loss). Add "do not finish heavier than you started".
- A plausible app number for "during" (my inference, not a guideline): ~0.4–0.8 L/h (ACSM's generic starting range), flagged as individual. Elite futsal players drank ~1.6 L per match (ad libitum), which corresponds to longer real-time durations (40 min stopped clock ≈ 75–90 min real).
- Dehydration correlates with minutes played → players with more minutes or in hot courts should drink more deliberately.

### Gaps
- No futsal study tested drinking to thirst vs planned drinking on performance.
- All futsal match data come essentially from one Spanish research group, one elite team, temperate conditions (22–26 °C); no data from hot/humid amateur courts.

## 3. After exercise: 125–150% of losses, sodium

### Takeaway
Replace ~125–150% of the fluid lost (1.25–1.5 L per kg lost) when rapid rehydration is needed, with sodium from food or drink; don't restrict salt after heavy sweating. UEFA notes that in most situations normal eating and drinking suffice. Refuel within 2 h.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM/AND/DC 2016: "effective rehydration requires the intake of a greater volume of fluid (e.g., 125%–150%) than the final fluid deficit (e.g., 1.25–1.5 L fluid for every 1 kg BW lost)." Rehydration should "involve the consumption of water and sodium at a modest rate that minimizes diuresis/urinary losses"; athletes "should not be advised to restrict sodium in their post-exercise nutrition particularly when large sodium losses have been incurred." Excess alcohol discouraged. — [Thomas et al. 2016](https://www.dietitians.ca/DietitiansOfCanada/media/Documents/Resources/noap-position-paper.pdf)
- ACSM 2007: after exercise "the goal is to replace any fluid electrolyte deficit. The speed with which rehydration is needed and the magnitude of fluid electrolyte deficits will determine if an aggressive replacement program is merited." (The commonly cited "~1.5 L per kg lost" figure is attributed to this paper; not verified in the text I read.) — [Sawka et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17277604/)
- NATA 2017: "refuel (ie, eat and drink) within 2 hours of physical activity to replace fluid, electrolytes, carbohydrates, and protein" (SORT B); diet and rehydration beverages should include "sufficient sodium (enough to replace losses but not an excessive amount)" (SORT B); beverages ≤4% alcohol do not dehydrate, higher alcohol content should be discouraged (SORT B). — [McDermott et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5634236/)
- UEFA 2021: players should reduce fluid/electrolyte deficit soon after the match, "however, in most situations there is sufficient opportunity and time to restore euhydration and electrolyte balance with normal eating/drinking practices." Alcohol impairs rehydration. — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- EFSA 2010: "Loss of body weight, denoting loss of body water, of about 1% is normally compensated within 24 hours." — [EFSA DRV summary report](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2017_09_DRVs_summary_report.pdf)

### Inferences
- App rule: if the user weighs before/after, post-exercise fluid target = 1.25–1.5 × kg lost (e.g., −1 kg → 1.25–1.5 L over the following hours), spread with the post-session meal (which supplies sodium). If not weighed, "drink with the recovery meal until urine is pale".
- With typical futsal losses of ~0.5–1% BM (≈0.4–0.8 kg for 75 kg), the post-match target would be ~0.5–1.2 L above normal — modest, consistent with UEFA's "normal eating/drinking usually suffices".
- Specific post-exercise sodium amount (mg) is not given numerically by any statement → app should not invent one; express as "include a salty food in the recovery meal".

### Gaps
- No numeric post-exercise sodium target (mg or mmol/L) in ACSM 2016, NATA 2017 or UEFA 2021.

## 4. Sweat rates and sodium losses measured in futsal; prevalence of dehydration in indoor courts

### Takeaway
Futsal sweat rates range roughly 1.1 L/h (pro training) to ~2.6 L/h of playing time (elite matches); total match sweat ~2.2–2.5 L in elite field players. Ad libitum drinking replaces ~57–80% → mean losses ~0.5–1.6% BM; few players exceed 2%. I found **no study measuring sweat sodium in adult futsal players**; values must be borrowed from football (~28–52 mmol/L ≈ 650–1200 mg/L). Pre-session hypohydration is common in team sports generally.

### Cited Findings
- Elite futsal matches: sweat loss 2.2–2.5 L per match, intake 1.2–1.7 L, deficit ~0.6–1.3% (see section 2). — [García-Jiménez et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.14198/jhse.2015.104.05); [2011](https://scispace.com/pdf/hydration-habits-in-elite-futsal-players-during-official-4xdbche5yx.pdf); [2014](https://doi.org/10.12691/ajssm-2-3-4)
- Range of individual dehydration in elite matches: −1.31% (gain) to 3.02%. — [García-Jiménez et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.14198/jhse.2015.104.05)
- Pro futsal training (Brazil): 1.13 ± 0.53 L/h, −0.52% BM. — [Wilke et al. 2016 JSCR](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2016/08000/metabolic_demand_and_internal_training_load_in.32.aspx)
- **University (amateur-competitive) futsal, Brazil** (17 male, two trainings, 360 mL water 40 min before, then no drinking allowed): water loss 1.02 ± 0.28 kg and 1.18 ± 0.44 kg; dehydration 1.40 ± 0.38% and 1.59 ± 0.56% (max 2.68%); sweat rate 14.5 ± 3.9 and 18.2 ± 6.8 mL/min (≈0.9–1.1 L/h, my conversion). Most athletes had "inadequate hydration habits." Note: drinking was prohibited, so this shows losses without replacement. — [Sepeda, Mendes, Loureiro, Rev Bras Med Esporte (SciELO)](https://www.scielo.br/j/rbme/a/33mbthQx844X3YghXkFWjFr/abstract/?lang=en) (year/DOI not captured)
- Amateur selection, Argentina (15 players 18–30 y, national tournament Oct 2023): most lost <1 kg; 50% had sweat rate <1 L/h; authors stress variability. Undergraduate thesis, low evidence weight. — [Sánchez 2024, Univ. (rid.ugr.edu.ar)](https://rid.ugr.edu.ar/handle/20.500.14125/970)
- Youth futsal (25 boys, 11 y): sweat rate 9.4 ± 5.0 mL/min (2.5–24.4), mean −0.5 ± 1.4% BM (range −2.5% to +4.3%); 9 of 25 gained weight. Not applicable to adults. — [Navarro et al., Rev Bras Nutr Esportiva (Dialnet PDF)](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/5693301.pdf)
- Sweat sodium proxy — football:
  - Elite female soccer (FC Barcelona): sweat [Na+] 28 ± 10 mmol/L training, 35 ± 9 match; sweat rate 0.47 L/h training, 0.98 L/h match; Na lost 525 mg (training) vs 1302 ± 689 mg (match). — [Tarnowski et al. 2022, Nutrients 14(15):3188](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/14/15/3188)
  - Pro male soccer, hot Colombia (32 °C, 79% RH): predicted whole-body sweat Na 43 ± 15.9 mmol/L; SR 1.7 ± 0.5 L/h; 40.6% lost ≥1% BM. — [Suarez-Ortegón et al. 2024, Medicina (PMC10819496)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10819496/)
  - Collegiate male soccer, China: whole-body sweat Na 52.2 ± 10.4 mmol/L (range 31–81); WBSR 0.65 L/h (cool practice) to 1.74 L/h (hot game); NaCl lost 2.1–4.75 g/session; >50% hypohydrated pre-exercise. — [Ma et al. 2025, Sci Rep 15:43899](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12708657/)
  - Range across soccer and other sports cited there: 12.6–104.8 mmol/L. — [Ma et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12708657/)
- ACSM/AND/DC 2016: average sweat sodium "approximates 50 mmol/L (~1 g/L)", highly variable. — [Thomas et al. 2016](http://drugfreesport.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Position-stand-on-Nutrition-Athletic-Performance-ACSM-2016-1.pdf)
- NATA 2017: heat acclimatisation increases sweat losses and lowers sweat electrolyte concentration (SORT A). — [McDermott et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5634236/)

### Inferences
- A rough sodium loss estimate for an amateur futsal session: 1 L sweat × ~0.65–1.2 g Na/L ≈ 0.6–1.2 g sodium — a normal salted meal covers this; supports "salty food after" rather than supplements.
- Indoor courts are not necessarily cool (Spanish data 22–26 °C); summer/heated pavilions or poorly ventilated amateur courts are an unmeasured risk.

### Gaps
- **No adult futsal sweat-sodium study found** (searched PubMed/SciELO terms). Values are extrapolated from football.
- No futsal study on pre-session hypohydration prevalence (USG) in adult amateurs.
- Sepeda et al., Navarro et al., RICYDE and Archivos papers: year/DOI not captured.

## 5. Simple self-assessment methods for amateurs (weighing, urine colour)

### Takeaway
Weighing nude/minimal clothing before and after training (corrected for drink intake and urine) is the recommended practical method to estimate sweat rate; urine colour, thirst and void frequency are acceptable personal cues (lower-grade evidence). First-morning urine is more reliable than spot samples. Morning body weight is a daily check.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM 2007: "Individual sweat rates can be estimated by measuring body weight before and after exercise." — [Sawka et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17277604/)
- Calculation used in futsal studies: total sweat = (pre − post body mass) + fluid ingested (− urine); sweat rate = total sweat / duration; %BM change = (post − pre)/pre × 100. — [Wilke et al. 2016](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2016/08000/metabolic_demand_and_internal_training_load_in.32.aspx)
- NATA 2017: body-mass change is "a quick and effective way" to track hydration over 24 h; valid only against ≥3 consecutive days of euhydrated baseline mass (SORT B). Educate athletes on sweat-rate calculation and individualised plans (SORT B). — [McDermott et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5634236/)
- NATA 2017: "Thirst sensation, void frequency, and urine color are valuable indicators over the course of a day or between days" (SORT C); first-morning urine increases validity; spot samples can mislead (SORT B). — [McDermott et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5634236/)
- ACSM/AND/DC 2016: track early-morning body weight (after waking and voiding); first-morning USG <1.020 (perhaps up to <1.025) indicates euhydration; urine osmolality >900 mOsmol/kg hypohydration, <700 euhydration. — [Thomas et al. 2016](http://drugfreesport.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Position-stand-on-Nutrition-Athletic-Performance-ACSM-2016-1.pdf)
- UEFA 2021: daily BM, thirst, urine colour, osmolality and USG are useful indicators; USG <1.020 suggests euhydration. — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- Pre-exercise target urine colour: "pale yellow" (ACSM 2016, UEFA 2021). — sources above.

### Inferences
- App feature: optional "weigh before/after" input → compute % loss and sweat rate (code, not AI), then post-exercise target = 1.25–1.5 × kg lost; warn if weight gained (over-drinking). This is simple arithmetic suitable for a rules engine.
- Urine colour as a qualitative daily check ("pale yellow = OK") for rest/light days.

### Gaps
- No validated urine-colour chart cut-off is given in these statements (Armstrong chart not fetched).

## 6. Electrolytes and sports drinks for 60–90 min sessions

### Takeaway
For ≤60–90 min in temperate conditions, water is generally sufficient; sodium-containing drinks are recommended for high sweat rates (>1.2 L/h), "salty sweaters", heat, or >2 h. Reference composition: ~20–30 mmol/L sodium (460–690 mg/L), 2–5 mmol/L potassium, 5–10% (ideally ≤8%) carbohydrate. In football, UEFA recommends ~30–60 g CHO/h for matches, which is a fuelling rather than hydration rationale.

### Cited Findings
- ACSM/AND/DC 2016: "Sodium should be ingested during exercise when large sweat sodium losses occur. Scenarios include athletes with high sweat rates (>1.2 L/h), 'salty sweat,' or prolonged exercise exceeding 2 hours." — [Thomas et al. 2016](http://drugfreesport.org.za/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/Position-stand-on-Nutrition-Athletic-Performance-ACSM-2016-1.pdf)
- ACSM 2007 (citing IOM): sports beverages "might contain ~20–30 meq/L sodium (chloride as the anion), ~2–5 meq/L potassium and ~5–10% carbohydrate"; for combined fluid+CHO, concentration "should not exceed 8%"; 0.5–1 L/h of a 6–8% drink gives 30–80 g CHO/h; CHO useful in high-intensity events of ~1 h or longer. — [Sawka et al. 2007](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/717055_6)
- NATA 2017: sodium supplementation before/during exercise should be individualised and practised (SORT B); cool, flavoured beverages promote intake (SORT B). — [McDermott et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5634236/)
- UEFA 2021: ~30–60 g CHO after warm-up and at half-time for matches; stoppages give extra drinking opportunities. — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- Pro futsal players offered water and a 6% CHO drink ad libitum chose mostly water (1.02 vs 0.31 L). — [Wilke et al. 2016](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2016/08000/metabolic_demand_and_internal_training_load_in.32.aspx)
- Exercise-associated hyponatremia (EAH) is caused by excessive hypotonic fluids "including sports drinks"; "uncommon in team-sport athletes." — [McDermott et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5634236/)

### Inferences
- App rule: light training/rest → water; hard training ≤90 min temperate → water (sports drink optional); match or hot court or measured sweat rate >1.2 L/h → drink with sodium (or water + salty snack). Keeps the app away from pushing commercial products.
- A homemade drink can be checked by code against 460–690 mg Na/L and ≤8% CHO.

### Gaps
- No futsal-specific trial on sports drinks vs water for 40-min (stopped clock) matches in amateurs.

## 7. Daily baseline intake (EFSA 2010)

### Takeaway
EFSA Adequate Intake for total water (drinks + food moisture): 2.5 L/day men, 2.0 L/day women, valid only at moderate temperature and moderate activity (PAL 1.6). Training/match losses must be added on top. Food typically supplies ~20–30% (NNR: 1–1.5 L/day).

### Cited Findings
- EFSA 2010: AIs "2.0 L/day (P95 3.1 L) for females and 2.5 L/day (P95 4.0 L) for males"; include "drinking water, beverages of all kind, and from food moisture"; "only apply to conditions of moderate environmental temperature and moderate physical activity levels (PAL 1.6)". Adolescents ≥14 y use adult values. — [EFSA NDA Panel 2010, DOI 10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1459](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2010.1459)
- EFSA: losses under extreme temperature and exercise "can be up to about 8,000 mL/day" and must be replaced with electrolytes; kidney max excretion ~0.7–1.0 L/h, overconsumption beyond it risks hyponatraemic water intoxication. — [EFSA DRV summary report](https://www.efsa.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2017_09_DRVs_summary_report.pdf)
- Contradiction on food inclusion: the EU Knowledge4Policy table footnote says the EFSA values "include water and other beverages only, not water derived from foods" — this contradicts the EFSA opinion itself, which explicitly includes food moisture. Trust the primary EFSA text. The same page notes NNR 2012: foods provide 1000–1500 mL/day. — [Knowledge4Policy](https://knowledge-for-policy.ec.europa.eu/health-promotion-knowledge-gateway/defining-water-recommendations-3_en)

### Inferences
- App baseline: rest day = EFSA AI (2.5/2.0 L total; ~70–80% from drinks ≈ 1.8–2.0 L men / 1.4–1.6 L women — my arithmetic assuming 20–30% from food); training/match days = baseline + estimated sweat loss (default ~1 L/h of activity if not measured, from futsal data 1.1 L/h training; or measured value).

### Gaps
- EFSA gives no per-kg figure or adjustment formula for athletes.

## 8. Do the figures apply to amateurs or only elite?

### Takeaway
Position stands (ACSM 2007/2016, NATA 2017) target "physically active individuals" including recreational exercisers; UEFA 2021 is explicitly elite. Futsal match data are elite (Spanish LNFS); amateur adult futsal data are scarce (one Brazilian university study, one Argentine thesis). Amateurs likely sweat less per hour (lower intensity) but may have worse habits.

### Cited Findings
- NATA 2017 covers both elite athletes and recreational exercisers. — [NATA press release](https://www.nata.org/NR10062017); [McDermott et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5634236/)
- ACSM/AND/DC 2016 outlines "recommendations for active adults and competitive athletes"; advises referral to a registered dietitian for personalised plans. — [Dietitians of Canada](https://www.dietitians.ca/DietitiansOfCanada/media/Documents/Resources/noap-position-paper.pdf)
- UEFA 2021 is titled "nutrition in elite football". — [Collins et al. 2021](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/55/8/416)
- Kenefick 2018: for people "less concerned with performance or performing activities at lower intensities, particularly in cooler weather, a fluid replacement plan may not be as important because fluid losses may not approach 2% body mass loss." — [Kenefick 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5790864/)
- University futsal players had "inadequate hydration habits" despite knowledge. — [Sepeda et al., RBME](https://www.scielo.br/j/rbme/a/33mbthQx844X3YghXkFWjFr/abstract/?lang=en)
- NATA 2017: beyond age 50 thirst sensitivity decreases (SORT A) — relevant to veteran amateur leagues. — [McDermott et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5634236/)

### Inferences
- Generic thresholds (<2% loss, no weight gain, 5–10 mL/kg pre, 125–150% post, EFSA baseline) are safe to apply to healthy adult amateurs. Elite-derived volumes (e.g., ~1.6 L/match) should not be used as a default for amateurs.

### Gaps
- No controlled futsal study in adult amateur players with fluid intake allowed.

## Summary of consensus and contradictions (for the report writer)
- **High consensus:** avoid >2% BM loss (UEFA relaxes to 2–3%); never gain weight during exercise (NATA SORT A); individualise via weighing; pre-exercise 5–10 mL/kg 2–4 h; post-exercise 125–150% of deficit with sodium; EFSA 2.5/2.0 L/day baseline.
- **Contradiction 1:** drink to thirst (Goulet 2012/2019; Kenefick for <90 min cool) vs planned drinking for team sports (UEFA 2021; ACSM 2007 customised programmes). Endurance meta-analyses don't test team-sport skills.
- **Contradiction 2:** Goulet 2012 argues the 2% rule doesn't apply to real-world self-paced exercise; ACSM/NATA retain it.
- **Contradiction 3:** pre-exercise volume 5–10 mL/kg (ACSM 2016) vs 5–7 mL/kg (UEFA 2021).
- **Contradiction 4:** Knowledge4Policy footnote says EFSA AI excludes food water; EFSA primary text includes it.
- **Futsal-specific:** rolling substitutions + ad libitum drinking keep elite players at ~1% loss in 22–26 °C; minutes played predicts dehydration; no futsal sweat-sodium data.
- All rules must be labelled "pendiente de validar por un dietista-nutricionista" per project rules.
